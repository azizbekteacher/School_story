# Class turnarounds: documented first-day and single-lesson wins

Research file for chapter 2 (Birinchi dars: 5-B) - one 45-minute lesson with
fourteen strangers. Everything below is tagged with how much it can be trusted.

Compiled by fetching pages directly (web_search is broken in this environment).

---

## 0. VERIFICATION LEGEND - read this before using anything

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[PEER-REVIEWED]** | Published study with numbers. Strongest. |
| **[FIRST-HAND]** | The teacher describing their own classroom, named, in a published piece. |
| **[JOURNALISM]** | A named outlet reporting on a named teacher. |
| **[SECOND-HAND]** | A student or colleague describing what the teacher did. |
| **[PRACTITIONER]** | A working teacher's blog about their own practice. Real, but not audited. No independent check. |
| **[FOLKLORE]** | Circulating story, no traceable origin, no verifiable teacher. Usable as *shape*, never as evidence. |

A blunt warning up front: **the internet's supply of "teacher walks into hostile
class and wins them over in one lesson" stories is overwhelmingly folklore.**
What genuinely exists, in verifiable form, is (a) one strong randomised study of
door greetings, (b) a small number of named teachers describing their own first
week in concrete detail, and (c) a large number of practitioner blogs that are
honest but unaudited. Anyone who tells you they found ten documented
single-lesson turnarounds with named teachers and quotes is probably counting
folklore.

### #1 - The index card: five questions, read before the next lesson
**Teacher:** Marcus Luther, high school English, Pacific Northwest (previously
eight years in Arkansas). **Tag: [FIRST-HAND]** - he describes his own practice
in his own words.

**Source:** https://www.edutopia.org/article/a-first-week-survey-that-fits-on-an-index-card
(and the podcast https://www.edutopia.org/podcast/how-to-jumpstart-belonging-in-your-classroom-from-day-one)

**What the class was like:** Not described as hostile. The problem he names is
the ordinary one: a new teacher with "a lot of new students to get to know" and
"so much going on during that first week" that surveys go unread.

**Exactly what he did.** One index card, five questions, and - the whole point -
**short enough that he reads every single card before those students walk in the
next day.**

1. "What is your top value and why?" Students narrow from a broad list of core
   values to one, write it, then share in small groups then whole group.
2. "What has been your experience with school up to this point, 1-7?" 1 = "very
   negative and/or frustrating", 7 = "incredibly positive and supportive."
   **Not shared aloud. Not required to explain the number.** He first names, out
   loud, that some of them have had genuinely harmful experiences of school.
   A week later he shows the class the anonymous distribution of all answers -
   and every single number is represented, in a room of students who have been
   in class together for years.
3. "What matters most to you from a classroom and teacher?" Six options, all
   C-words: consistency, change, challenge, compassion, clarity, creativity.
   Individual answer, then a show of hands per word ("informal class poll"), then
   pair debrief. He closes by pointing out that some of these are the exact
   opposite of each other, and the room will need to be different things at
   different times.
4. "What is an interesting or boring fact about yourself?" He models both - his
   interesting fact is **driving into a wall the first day he got his driving
   licence**; his boring fact is having two older brothers. Students jot
   silently, then get up and tell three people they do not know, saying their
   name each time.
5. Optional: "What is helpful for me to know to better support you right away in
   this classroom?" He is explicit that the wording matters and warns against
   the over-reaching versions ("Tell me something really important about you").
   He keeps examples narrow and classroom-shaped: seating, noise, name
   preferences.

**What changed:** He frames the payoff as community, not compliance - students
realise their classmates' school experience was not their own, and the teacher
has one specific true thing to say to each of them later.

**Why this is the single most usable item on the list:** it is one card, one
pen, no budget, and it fits in the first ten minutes and the last five.

**Caveat:** Luther does not claim this fixed a hostile class. It is a
first-week relationship move with a thoughtful design, not a turnaround
narrative. Do not oversell it.

### #2 - The 45-second one-to-one, with the survey highlighted
**Teacher:** An Edutopia contributor describing her own classroom (byline on the
article; she has run this "experiment" since about 2017). **Tag: [FIRST-HAND]**.

**Source:** https://www.edutopia.org/article/building-relationships-students-first-week
("The Power of a 45-Second Investment in Relationship Building", 23 June 2023)

**What the class was like:** In her words, the fear is the ordinary first-day
one: "the fear of this happening in real life has caused me to be overprepared."
She describes August teacher dreams about "me yelling so that the kids around
the corner and down the stairs behind the column can hear me."

**Exactly what she did, day one:**
- Greets **at the door, with a smile**, and tells them there is a seating chart
  and a survey to start on - "this eases anxiety about where to sit and gives
  them something to do right away." She hands out pencils as needed.
- **Music playing** while they fill the survey - "to alleviate awkwardness."
- She does not do a whole-class introduction. She says she will **walk around
  and quietly introduce herself to each of them**, and asks them to give first
  AND last name, so she can write it phonetically on the seating chart and
  **never mispronounce it in front of the room.**
- Then a short presentation about herself **with pictures**: she has been
  skydiving (never again), ran a marathon over a decade ago, plays Ultimate
  Frisbee, and shows a photo of her young kids "helping" her set up.
- Ends the lesson with name games.

**The move that actually does the work, in week one:** she assigns something
simple and fun, sits at the **back** of the room with music on, and calls each
student over one at a time. She has their survey in front of her with **one
thing highlighted** - something she has in common, or something she wants to
know more about. Her own example lines:

> "Oh, I really enjoy reading, too!" / "Yeah, I had older siblings growing up as
> well" / "What's an ollie trick?"

**What changed, in her words:** "Students feel valued, respected, and seen by
you, which means they are less likely to act out." And the mechanism she names
for a hostile kid is the important one: "It allows for a clean slate to build a
positive relationship with you, **regardless of whatever reputation they came in
with**."

**Caveat:** 45 seconds per student, 30 students = 22 minutes. That is most of a
45-minute lesson. This is a week-one move spread across two lessons, not a
single-lesson move, unless the class is small. For a class of fourteen it is
about 11 minutes - which fits.

### #3 - Positive Greetings at the Door (PGD): the one with numbers
**Researchers:** Cook, Fiat, Larson, Daikos, Slemrod, Holland, Thayer, Renshaw
(2018), *Journal of Positive Behavior Interventions* 20(3), 149-159.
**Tag: [PEER-REVIEWED]** - randomised, with a blind observer.

**Source (summary with the numbers):** https://my.chartered.college/research-hub/positive-greetings-at-the-door/
**Source (original):** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1098300717753831

**What the classes were like:** Ten teachers, 203 students, two middle schools in
the Pacific Northwest, six language arts and four maths classes - **chosen
specifically because they were "typified by a high rate of disruptive and
off-task behaviors."** These are the difficult classes, not a convenient sample.
Classes were observed, matched into pairs by baseline, then randomly assigned to
PGD or an attention control.

**Exactly what the teachers did.** Trained in two one-hour sessions. The
authors' own implementation checklist, from the study summary at
https://spreadingkindnesscampaign.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/Positive-Greetings-at-the-Door.pdf
(cost stated there in one word: **"Cost: free"**):
1. **"Specific positive interactions (i.e., greeting using the student's name,
   handshake) with each student as they enter the classroom, in order to increase
   social belonging and promote a positive classroom climate."** Not a general
   "morning, everyone." Each one, by name, at the threshold.
2. **Pre-corrective statements, to individual students and to the whole class** -
   "reminding the student of the behaviors that will result in success, **prior to
   the students beginning classroom activities**." Said on the way in, not after
   the first disruption.
3. **A structured learning activity already prepared and ready as they walk
   in.** This is part of the pre-correction package in the study, and it is the
   same move the maths teacher in #8 arrives at independently. The room must not
   be waiting for instructions.
4. **Pre-corrective, encouraging messages to specific students who had struggled
   with their behaviour the previous day** - a quiet word aimed at the kids most
   likely to fail today, delivered before they have a chance to.
5. **Behaviour-specific praise** to reinforce what they wanted repeated.

**What changed (measured, not remembered):**
- Academic engaged time: **58.75% -> 79.70%** (control stayed flat, ~54%).
- Disruptive behaviour: **13.68% -> 4.13%** of class time (control 15.3% ->
  12.53%).
- Teachers implemented it with 96% adherence, and rated it 5.5 out of 6 on
  acceptability. **Cost: zero.**

**Honest limits, stated by the reviewers themselves:** the design cannot say
*which* of the components did the work; there was no follow-up to see if the
gains held; the sample was small and from two similar settings. To that add one
the study itself supplies: the teachers had **"low baseline levels of academic
engagement and classroom management practices"** - in other words the teachers,
not just the classes, started badly. That cuts both ways: it makes the result
more striking, and it means part of the gain may be the training, not the
greeting.

**Why it matters for the game:** this is the only item on this list where the
claim "it worked" is backed by an experiment rather than a memory. And the core
move is one sentence long, delivered at a doorway. It is the cheapest credible
turnaround mechanism that exists.

### #4 - Ask the shortest question you can, then shut up: the water tank
**Teacher:** Dan Meyer, then a high-school maths teacher, now well known for
Three-Act Math. **Tag: [FIRST-HAND]** - a TED talk in his own words with his own
classroom described.

**Source:** https://www.ted.com/talks/dan_meyer_math_curriculum_makeover/transcript

**What the class was like - his own description of the problem:** "I sell a
product to a market that doesn't want it, but is forced by law to buy it." He
lists five symptoms he was seeing: **lack of initiative** (students do not
self-start), **lack of perseverance**, **lack of retention**, **aversion to word
problems**, and the remaining students "eagerly looking for the formula to apply
in that situation."

**The diagnosis that makes it concrete:** textbooks hand over the visual, the
mathematical structure, and the sub-steps all at once. "We're paving a smooth,
straight path from one to the other and congratulating our students for how well
they can step over the small cracks in the way."

**Exactly what he did** - the water tank:
- Strips the textbook problem to a photograph of a real tank being filled, and
  one question: **"How long will it take to fill it up?"** Nothing else. No
  dimensions, no rate, no formula prompt.
- Then replaces the photo with **a video of someone actually filling it** -
  "filling up slowly, agonizingly slowly. It's tedious. Students are looking at
  their watches, rolling their eyes, and they're all wondering at some point or
  another, 'Man, how long is it going to take to fill up?'" His comment: "That's
  how you know you've baited the hook."
- Lets students decide what matters: "Does the height matter? Does the side of
  it matter? Does the colour of the valve matter?"
- **Writes their names on the board next to their guesses.** Public commitment,
  zero stakes.
- Does not give the answer from the answer key. **Plays the end of the video.**
  And when the model and the measurement do not quite agree, he treats the gap
  as the lesson - "sources of error ... those conversations have been, among the
  most valuable."

**The specific psychological move, in his words:** "every student is on a level
playing field of intuition. Everyone's filled something up with water before, so
I get kids answering the question ... **I've got kids who are mathematically and
conversationally intimidated joining the conversation.**" He names the exclusion
mechanism he was defeating: "kids who will not join a conversation about math
because someone else has the formula ... so I won't talk about it."

**What changed:** "one semester in, I can put something on the board, totally
new, totally foreign, and they'll have a conversation about it for three or four
minutes more than they would have at the start of the year." Note the honest
timescale: **a semester, not a lesson**, and the gain is measured in minutes of
argument.

**His own summary of the technique:** "ask the shortest question you possibly
can and let those more specific questions come out in conversation ... and to
finally, in total, just be less helpful."

**Caveat for an Uzbek classroom with no budget:** the video is the one thing
that costs something. It does not have to be a video. The mechanism is
**one unanswerable-looking question about something physical that everyone has
seen**, plus a visible commitment of a guess, plus letting the room argue before
you give them a method.

### #5 - Name the thing in the first two minutes: fear vs trust
**Teacher:** Rafe Esquith, Hobart Elementary, Los Angeles, Room 56. **Tag:
[FIRST-HAND]** - from his own book, in his own words, with his own failures
included.

**Source:** *Teach Like Your Hair's on Fire* (full text):
https://archive.org/download/rafe2/rafe2.pdf

**What he admits he used to do - and this is the useful part:** "In my early
years, I actually planned to frighten the kids the first day of school. I wanted
to make sure they knew I was boss. Some of my colleagues did the same, and we
shared our supposed successes in getting the kids in order."

**His diagnosis of the ordinary classroom:** "The simple truth is that most
classrooms today are managed by one thing and one thing only: fear. The teacher
is afraid: afraid of looking bad, of not being liked, of not being listened to,
of losing control. The students are even more afraid."

**Exactly what he does instead, and the timing is the technique:** "**On the
first day of school, within the first two minutes**, I discuss this issue with
the children. While most classrooms are based on fear, our classroom is based on
trust." He does not wait for a behaviour to correct. He says the rule before
anyone can break it.

**The concrete illustration he uses with them, day one:** the trust-fall. "Even
if the catch is made a hundred times in a row, the trust is broken forever if
the friend lets you fall the next time as a joke." His stated rule: "My students
learn the first day that **a broken trust is irreparable. Everything else can be
fixed.** Miss your homework assignment? Just tell me ... Did you break something?
It happens ... But break my trust and the rules change."

**The second half of the move, which is what makes it credible rather than
sentimental:** he then spends the year being literally dependable. "If I tell the
kids we are beginning a special art project on Friday, I have to deliver, even if
it means running out to a twenty-four-hour Home Depot at 4:00 A.M." And he tells
a story about a colleague who promised a class trip all year, used it as a
threat, and then announced in the last week that she was moving away and could
not take them. "This betrayal not only ruined anything good she had done with
the kids that year, but soured many of them on school and adults in general."

**The third piece, discipline logic:** "Children do not mind a tough teacher, but
they despise an unfair one." His example of the illogical punishment: John does
not do his maths homework, so he misses art. "There is no connection here." In
Room 56 the punishment is always the loss of the activity the misbehaviour
happened in - and it is restorable: "You can watch the experiment but you may not
participate. You will have another chance tomorrow."

**What changed:** he describes Room 56 as "an oasis" that visitors notice for its
calm rather than its test scores - "Room 56 is a special place not because of
what it has, but because of what it is missing: fear."

**Honest caveat:** this is a memoir, not a study, and it is a year-long culture,
not a 45-minute fix. What is usable in one lesson is narrow and real: **say the
rule out loud, in the first two minutes, before anyone tests it, and make it
about trust rather than about consequences.** Also note Esquith's own honesty
about the cost: "It's not easy to create a classroom without fear. It can take
years."

### #6 - The reward economy: they get paid, and they pay rent
**Teacher:** Rafe Esquith again. **Tag: [FIRST-HAND]** for the system; the
commercial "My Classroom Economy" adaptation is documented separately.

**Sources:** https://archive.org/download/rafe2/rafe2.pdf ;
https://room360.org/classroom-economy/ ;
https://next.jumpstartclearinghouse.org/resource/my-classroom-economy/

**The mechanism:** a running classroom economy. Students hold **jobs**, earn a
**paycheque**, and **pay rent on their own desk**. The Vanguard Group built a
free published curriculum around Esquith's system ("My Classroom Economy"), which
is the version most teachers copy. The design point is not the prizes - it is
that the money is **earned, recurring, and spent on something the student already
needs** (their seat), so the classroom starts to feel like a place with stakes.

**Esquith's own warning about rewards, which is the most useful sentence in this
whole file for your chapter:** he argues that promising a child a reward for good
behaviour "in itself is a problem" - and that the far worse failure is the adult
who breaks the promise. His illustration is the teacher who dangled a trip all
year as a threat and then cancelled it. **The gift is only worth something if the
giver is dependable.**

**How to use this in a chapter where the teacher announces small gifts for
correct answers:** the honest version is not "answers earn prizes." It is
"answers earn something small, and the teacher keeps the promise even when it is
inconvenient." The second half is the part that changes a class. The first half
alone is a vending machine.

**Cost/feasibility for a real Uzbek maths teacher:** a full classroom economy is
a term-long build, not a Monday move. The 45-minute version is much smaller:
a visible tally, a named small prize, and a promise kept. See section 4.

### #7 - Intercept the ugly thing and make it the curriculum
**Teacher:** Erin Gruwell, Wilson High School, Long Beach, Room 203, 1994.
**Tag: [SECOND-HAND]/[JOURNALISM]** - documented in her own published diary and
by outlets, but the classroom account is retrospective and partly
mythologised by the 2007 film.

**Sources:** https://www.library.rochester.edu/about/news/neilly-author-series-erin-gruwell ;
https://inside.heidelberg.edu/news/091622/hypes-erin-gruwell-students-write-your-own-ending ;
https://www.litcharts.com/lit/the-freedom-writers-diary/summary

**What the class was like:** the Freedom Writers Foundation's own summary of day
one is unusually blunt: "On their first day of high school, Erin Gruwell's
students had only three things in common: **they hated school, they hated each
other, and they hated her.**" She was 23, a first-year teacher, and the class was
officially a "lost cause" - tracked as unteachable.

**Exactly what she did - and note it was NOT day one:** after months of failing
to reach them, **she intercepted a racist caricature being passed around the
room** - a drawing of a Black student with exaggerated features. She did not
confiscate it and move on. She held it up in front of the class and **drew the
comparison to the propaganda of Nazi Germany**. (The Heidelberg University
account, linked above, is the source for that sequence.) The often-repeated
follow-on - that she then asked the room who knew what the Holocaust was, and
almost nobody did, and that gap became the year's curriculum - is consistent
across accounts but I could not open a primary document for it, so treat the
specific detail as **reported, not confirmed**.

**What changed:** a class tracked as unteachable became a class that read *The
Diary of Anne Frank* and wrote about it. The mechanism is the repeatable part:
**she took the weapon and turned it into the syllabus.** The thing they used to
wound each other became the thing the class studied.

**Honest caveat, and it matters:** this took months, not a lesson. The film
compresses it and invents scenes. Treat it as a *shape* - intercept, refuse to
punish, convert the object into content - not as a first-day recipe.

### #8 - The first five minutes are a routine, not an event
**Author:** a working middle-school maths teacher writing as "Maneuvering the
Middle". **Tag: [PRACTITIONER]** - explicitly her own practice.

**Source:** https://www.maneuveringthemiddle.com/no-time-wasted-the-first-and-last-5-minutes-of-class/

**Her credential, in her words:** "I have lived through at least 7,500 class
periods."

**The claim, and it is the one worth stealing:** "The class periods that had the
highest mastery, where my students were the happiest, and when I felt the most
calm and effective as a teacher all had one thing in common: **a peaceful start
to the class.**" And the reason it is a first-day issue and not a week-six
issue: "it is hard to reset a class period after a rough start."

**Exactly what her first five minutes are:**
- She greets at the door. The student grabs the warm-up handout **on the way in**,
  goes straight to their seat, gets out a pencil, starts.
- **The same thing every single day.** "I didn't want there ever to be confusion
  about what a student should be doing."
- **Directions are always already on the board.** "The warm-up shouldn't require
  instruction or directions from me." This is the load-bearing detail: it means
  the teacher is free at the door and the room self-starts.
- Her two non-negotiables with students: (1) if you need me, sit down, start the
  warm-up, **raise your hand** - "I will only come to you if you are actively
  working"; (2) seated and silent when the bell rings, with a **three-second
  countdown** if they are not.
- She is candid that this is enforced by "practice, a lot of sending rowdy
  students back in the hallway to try again, and **positive narration** - 'Marie,
  great job getting started right away!'"

**What changed:** a class that starts itself, and a teacher who is not giving
directions four times as students trickle in. Her stated reward is her own
composure, which she is honest about: "this may completely be something that I
value as a teacher."

### #9 - The last five minutes: minute paper, close the loop, metacognition
**Author:** James Lang (via Mohawk College's teaching blog summary).
**Tag: [PRACTITIONER]/[JOURNALISM]** - a named scholar's teaching advice,
summarised by a college teaching centre.

**Source:** https://www.mohawkcollege.ca/about/news/blogs/last-five-minutes-of-class

**The problem, named exactly:** "The last five minutes of class often degenerates
into a struggle between the teacher to just get through two/three/five/seven more
points, students eager to get out and get to their next class and those desperate
to ask a question."

**Four concrete closing moves:**
1. **The minute paper.** Two questions only: *What was the most important thing
   you learned? What question do you still have?*
2. **Closing connections.** Finish five minutes early and tell them **they can
   leave when they can name five ways today's content shows up outside this
   room.** Lang's own example is from Browning: "list five popular songs in which
   the 'speaker' clearly does not represent the voice of the singer." Vary the
   number (three) or the time (ten minutes).
3. **The metacognitive five.** Do not review content - **tell them how to study
   it.** Lang surveyed his class on how they studied, correlated it with midterm
   scores, and showed them the two lists side by side. Low scorers said "reviewed
   my notes", "reread the poems". High scorers said "wrote an outline", "rewrote
   my notes", "organised a timeline", "tested myself", "created flashcards".
4. **Close the loop.** "Circle back to how you started the class." If you opened
   with questions, close by returning to those questions and having students
   answer them. If you opened by testing prior knowledge, close by asking whether
   the lesson confirmed or contradicted what they thought.

**Why this matters psychologically:** the last five minutes are what the class
*leaves with*, and they are the only five minutes a student will rehearse on the
walk out. Lang's moves all convert those five minutes from surrender into a
summary the student produces themselves.

### #10 - FOLKLORE, flagged: "Mrs Thompson and Teddy Stoddard"
**Tag: [FOLKLORE] - do not use as evidence.**

**Sources:** https://frtommylane.com/stories/encouragement/teddy_stoddard.htm ;
https://ripplekindness.org/the-teddy-stoddard-story/ ;
https://mytowntutors.com/make-a-difference-the-teddy-stallard-story/

**The story:** a fifth-grade teacher tells her class on day one that she loves
them all the same, which is a lie, because Teddy is slumped in the front row.
She then reads his file, learns his mother died, and from that day teaches
children rather than subjects. He becomes a doctor and writes to her.

**Why it is folklore, concretely:**
- **The boy's surname is not stable.** It appears as **Stoddard** and as
  **Stallard** across the same handful of copies. A real name does not do that.
- Every version is a retyped devotional or motivational page. None cites a
  school, a year, a district, or a newspaper.
- It is most commonly attributed as a story **Wayne Dyer told**, not as a story
  anyone reported.
- The opening line - "she told the children a lie" - is a rhetorical device, not
  a memory.

**Verdict: usable as a shape (the teacher reads the file and changes one thing),
never as a citation.** If the game's hero does this, it should be presented as
something he *decided to do*, not something that famously worked.

## 2. RANKING: usable by a maths teacher, no budget, 45 minutes, real room

Ranked on one question only: **could Azizbek do this in period three tomorrow
with a piece of chalk and no money?**

| Rank | Move | Fits 45 min? | Cost | Risk | Why it ranks here |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Door greetings, by name, every student (PGD #3)** | Yes - 90 seconds | 0 | Nearly none | The only experimentally verified move here. One sentence at a doorway. Works on the exact population (disruptive maths classes). |
| **2** | **One hard question, no method given, then let them argue (#4)** | Yes - 15-25 min | 0 | Lesson may run long; you must resist answering | Turns the room from audience into arguers. "Level playing field of intuition" is the mechanism, and it is free. |
| **3** | **The index card (#1)** | Yes - 10 min | 0 | None | Gives the teacher fourteen true facts about fourteen strangers, today, that he can use for the rest of the lesson. |
| **4** | **Say the rule out loud in the first two minutes (#5)** | Yes - 2 min | 0 | Sounds like a speech if done badly | Frames the room before anyone tests it. Must be followed by a kept promise or it poisons the well. |
| **5** | **Board is already written when they walk in (#8)** | Yes | 0 | None | Removes the dead first three minutes where the class decides whether to listen. |
| **6** | **The last-five-minutes loop (#9)** | Yes | 0 | None | Highest ratio of effect to effort in the whole file. |
| **7** | **The 45-second one-to-one (#2)** | Only for a class of ~14 (11 min) | 0 | 45 sec x 30 = most of a lesson | Excellent for this chapter specifically - fourteen students is exactly the size where it fits. |
| **8** | **Intercept and convert (#7)** | No | 0 | High - requires nerve and timing | A year-shape, not a lesson-shape. Usable as one dramatic beat, not as a plan. |
| **9** | **Classroom economy (#6)** | No | Low but non-zero | High - promises to keep | Term-long build. The 45-minute version is just "a visible tally and a kept promise". |
| **10** | **Mrs Thompson (#10)** | - | - | - | Folklore. Not a plan. |

**Top three, with the reason stated plainly:**

1. **PGD door greetings.** It is the only item with a randomised trial behind it,
   on classes chosen *because* they were disruptive, in ordinary classrooms, at
   zero cost, in under two minutes. The numbers (58.75% -> 79.70% engaged;
   13.68% -> 4.13% disruptive) are the strongest evidence in this file.
2. **Dan Meyer's shortest question.** It is the only entry that directly attacks
   *maths* disengagement rather than general behaviour, and Meyer names the exact
   psychological mechanism - the kids who stay silent because someone else
   already has the formula. Free, repeatable, and it makes the room argue.
3. **Marcus Luther's index card.** Not because it turns a class around, but
   because it is the only move here that gives the teacher *material* - fourteen
   specific true things he can use in the last twenty minutes of the same lesson.
   It converts "I do not know these people" into "I know what this one values."

## 4. GIVING SOMETHING: the reward question, for the chapter's gift beat

The chapter needs the teacher to announce that correct answers earn small gifts.
Here is what the sources actually support, and what they warn against.

**Supported:**
- **PGD's fifth component is behaviour-specific praise** - a verbal reward,
  precisely aimed, costs nothing, and it is part of the only experimentally
  validated package in this file (#3). Praise that names the behaviour ("you
  started before I finished writing the chalk on the board") is a gift with no
  budget line.
- **Esquith's classroom economy** shows a working reward system at scale - but
  note its design: students **earn** a paycheque for a **job**, and **pay rent**
  on their own desk. The reward is structural and recurring, not a prize for a
  right answer (#6).
- **Luther's index card (#1)** is a gift that costs nothing and lands harder than
  a prize: he reads fourteen cards and then says one specific true thing to each
  student. Attention is the gift.

**Warned against, in Esquith's own words:**
- Promising a reward for good behaviour "in itself is a problem."
- The unforgivable version is **breaking the promise**. His example is the
  teacher who used a promised trip as a year-long threat and then cancelled it:
  "This betrayal not only ruined anything good she had done with the kids that
  year, but soured many of them on school and adults in general."

**The honest formulation for the chapter:** the interesting dramatic question is
not *will the gift motivate them* - it is *will he keep the promise when keeping
it costs him something.* A gift announced and delivered is a transaction. A gift
announced, then threatened by a shortage, then delivered anyway, is the beat
worth writing. That is also the only version the sources support.

**Practical constraint for a real Uzbek school:** a first-year teacher paying for
prizes out of his own salary is a real and slightly shameful detail worth
keeping. Esquith's Home Depot at 4 a.m. is the same shape - the adult absorbs
the cost, silently, and does not mention it. "We do not need to lecture the
children about how we came through on a promise; let them figure out that they
can trust us."

## 5. THE FIRST FIVE MINUTES AND THE LAST FIVE MINUTES

### First five minutes

What the sources agree on, with the specific mechanism each one names:

1. **The room decides before you speak.** The practitioner's line: "it is hard to
   reset a class period after a rough start" (#8). The PGD study's whole premise
   is that the transition *into* the room is the preventable moment - it is an
   "antecedent-based strategy", i.e. it acts before behaviour exists.
2. **Every student, individually, at the threshold** (#3). The measured effect of
   the package this act belongs to, was +21 points of engaged time - and the
   study cannot separate the greeting from the pre-corrections that travelled
   with it. The mechanism it implies: a student who has been greeted by name has
   already been treated as a person in front of the room, and disrupting is now
   more expensive for them.
3. **Work already visible, directions already on the board** (#8, #2). Both
   teachers independently arrive at the same rule: **the first task must need no
   explanation.** The Edutopia teacher's reason is psychological - "this eases
   anxiety about where to sit and gives them something to do right away." The
   maths teacher's reason is managerial - "The warm-up shouldn't require
   instruction or directions from me."
4. **Say the frame out loud early** (#5). Esquith does it "within the first two
   minutes". Not a rule recital - a statement of what kind of room this is.
5. **Never mispronounce a name** (#2 is explicit about this: first and last name,
   written phonetically, so as not to "risk embarrassing the student"). For a
   first-year teacher in an unfamiliar school this is not politeness, it is the
   first test of whether he is paying attention.
6. **Silence has to be asked for, not demanded** (#8): a three-second countdown,
   and then positive narration of the students who are already doing it.

**The synthesis for the game:** the first five minutes are not where you teach
anything. They are where the room finds out whether this teacher is a person who
notices them, and whether the next 40 minutes have a shape. Both answers are
given before the first sentence of content.

### Last five minutes

1. **The last five minutes set the tone for the *next* class, not this one.**
   This is the practitioner's sharpest observation (#8): if cleanup and dismissal
   are a scramble, "you are frustrated. This will inevitably affect your mood
   with your next class period." The last five minutes are where the teacher's
   own next lesson is won or lost.
2. **The student should produce the summary, not receive it** (#9). Minute paper:
   *most important thing you learned* + *what question do you still have*. Two
   questions, one slip of paper.
3. **Close the loop** (#9). Return to the question you opened with. If you opened
   with a guess on the board, the close is whether the guess was right. This is
   the cheapest structural move in the file and it is the one that makes a lesson
   feel like an argument rather than a broadcast.
4. **Tell them how to study it, not what to study** (#9). Lang's two lists -
   "reviewed my notes" versus "tested myself" - shown side by side, is a
   five-minute intervention with a measured correlation behind it.
5. **The teacher dismisses, not the bell** (#8), and dismissal is earned by having
   the space ready. This is a small ritual that makes the last minute belong to
   the teacher rather than to the corridor.

**The synthesis for the game:** the last five minutes are the only part of the
lesson the student rehearses on the way out. If the teacher gives them the summary,
they leave with his sentences. If he makes them say it, they leave with their own.

## 6. WHAT THIS MEANS FOR CHAPTER 2, IN ONE PARAGRAPH

The defensible version of a first-lesson turnaround is not a speech and not a
stunt. It is a sequence of small, cheap, individually unimpressive moves that
compound: be at the door and use their names; have the first task already on the
board so the room starts without being told; ask one question they can all have
an opinion about and then refuse to rescue them from it; let them commit to a
guess in public; and in the last five minutes make them say what they learned.
The evidence for the door and the greeting is experimental; the evidence for the
rest is the testimony of named teachers about their own rooms. Neither supports a
teacher who changes a hostile class in 45 minutes by force of personality - and
that is probably the more useful truth for a game about a first-year teacher who
is not yet good at this.
