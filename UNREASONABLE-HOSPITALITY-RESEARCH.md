## 1. The core framework

### 1.1 The hot-dog cart story — the book's founding anecdote **(a)**

It is in **Chapter 18, "Improvisational Hospitality"**, and it is not a parable; it happened
during service. Every detail matters because the story's whole force is that it was small,
cheap, and specific.

- **Who:** Guidara, then general manager of Eleven Madison Park, clearing appetiser plates.
- **The table:** "a fourtop of Europeans headed straight to the airport after their meal."
- **What he overheard:** "We've been everywhere! Daniel, Per Se, Momofuku, now Eleven Madison Park. The only thing we didn't eat was a street hot dog."
- **What he did:** "I dropped the dirty dishes off in the kitchen and ran out to buy a hot dog from Abraham, who manned the Sabrett's cart on our corner."
- **The hard part:** he had to talk the chef into it. "He looked at me like I'd gone crazy… I held my ground and told him to trust me—that it was important to me—and he finally agreed to cut the hot dog into four perfect pieces, adding a swoosh of mustard, a swoosh of ketchup, and perfect quenelles of sauerkraut and relish to each plate."
- **What he said:** "Before we brought out their final savory course, I admitted to the guests that I'd been eavesdropping: 'We're thrilled you chose us for your last meal in New York, but we didn't want you to go home with any culinary regrets.'"
- **The result:** "They freaked out." And: "before they left, each person at the table told me it was the highlight not only of the meal, but of their trip to New York. They'd be telling the story for the rest of their lives."
- **The city:** New York. **The restaurant:** Eleven Madison Park, then four stars.
- **Why it mattered to Guidara, in his own words:** "I had given away thousands of dishes, and many, many (many) thousands of dollars' worth of food by that point in my career, and yet I can confidently say that nobody had ever responded the way that table responded to that hot dog."

The detail a teacher should steal is not *hot dog*. It is **"the only thing we didn't eat was
X"** — the guest named their own gap. The hospitality was *listening*, not *spending*.

### 1.2 "Enlightened Hospitality" — and whose idea it was **(a)**

Guidara did not invent this. It is **Danny Meyer's** philosophy, and Guidara is scrupulous about
saying so (Meyer's own book is *Setting the Table*). Verbatim from Chapter 3:

> "The cornerstone of the company's culture was a philosophy Danny called **Enlightened Hospitality**, which upended traditional hierarchies by prioritizing the people who worked there over everything else, including the guests and the investors. This didn't mean the customer suffered; in fact, the opposite. Danny's big idea was to hire great people, treat them well, and invest deeply into their personal and professional growth, and they would take great care of the customers—which is exactly what they did."

The order is the point: **staff before guests before investors.** This is the inward-facing
half of the book and it is what makes §9 below possible.

### 1.3 The 95/5 rule — real, and the exact wording is not what the internet says **(a)**

Chapter 5, "Restaurant-Smart vs. Corporate-Smart":

> "**Manage 95% of your business down to the penny and the other 5% foolishly.**"

The illustration: at the Modern (MoMA), a family mentioned mid-meal that snow was falling and
they had never seen real snow. "Spur of the moment, I sent someone out to buy four brand-new
sleds. After their meal was over, we had a chauffeur-driven SUV whisk the whole family up to
Central Park for a special nightcap: a few hours of play in the freshly fallen snow. That 5
percent, spent 'foolishly' (really, with tremendous intention), allowed us to create those
special memories for our guests."

**Correction to a common paraphrase.** The popular version — "spend on the 5% of details that
create 95% of memorable experiences" — is **not** the book's line and inverts its logic. The
book's 95% is *ruthless economy*; the 5% is a budgeted, deliberate overspend. The two halves are
a single discipline: you cannot afford the sleds if you have not counted the pennies. **(a)** for
the quote, **(c)** for the observation that the paraphrase inverts it.

He is explicit that the 5% is also spent on staff, not only guests: "Wherever we could we worked
to minimize expensive turnover and the dreaded overtime. But then, a few times a year, I would
spend a truly obnoxious amount of money on an experience for the team."

### 1.4 "Service is black and white, hospitality is colour" — **handle with care** **(a)/(b)**

This is your game's stated theme, so it matters more than anything else in this report that I am
precise about it.

- **Verified in the book:** the *image* is there, and it is Guidara's own closing frame. From
  Chapter 1: "…everything, in other words, you need to **turn the world from black and white to
  color** for you, the people you work with, and the people you serve." **(a)**
- **Not verified:** the crisp aphorism **"Service is black and white. Hospitality is colour."**
  as a sentence in this book. I did not find that formulation anywhere in the text I read, and
  quoted searches did not surface it as a Guidara line. The service/hospitality *distinction* is
  unambiguously the book's thesis — the whole book turns on it — but the **balanced two-clause
  slogan appears to be a later distillation** (very likely by the game's author, or by a
  summariser), not a quotation. **(b)** for "the distinction is central", **(c)** for "the
  slogan is a distillation".
- **Practical consequence for the game:** it is fine to keep the line as your theme. Do not put
  it in Guidara's mouth as a quotation, and do not build a level whose lesson is "find the exact
  phrasing in the book". The book's own service/hospitality contrast is better taught by
  *behaviour*: service is what the guest asked for; hospitality is what they didn't.

### 1.6 "Being present vs. attentive" — verified, and it is a *measured* claim **(a)**

Chapter 16. Guidara waiting tables at Tribeca Grill as a management intern noticed the tip sheet
didn't add up:

> "I was first interested in a group of servers who were like Keanu Reeves manipulating the Matrix; they saw everything and were always one step ahead… they turned tables with ruthless efficiency."

But: "I quickly realized that this was the real A team. In many ways, these servers were less
excellent than their more proficient colleagues—their tables waited longer to order, and for
dessert menus, and for their checks. But when these less efficient servers were at the tables,
connecting with their guests, they were so focused on the interaction that the bonds they
created were much stronger."

The definition to put on a classroom wall:

> "I often describe 'being present' as **caring so much about what you're doing that you stop caring about everything you need to do next.**"

And the two-word distinction:

> "The first group was **attentive**; the second **paid attention**."

And the consequence: "We were no longer in the business of running an extraordinary restaurant;
we were now in the business of human connection." **(a)**

Note this is the book's *strongest* evidence because it is a number, not a sentiment: the less
efficient servers earned more. Azizbek can be shown the same thing — the teacher who finishes
the syllabus on time versus the one who notices the boy at the back.

### 1.7 "The answer is yes — what is the question?" — **NOT IN THE BOOK** **(a)**

I found no version of this in the text or index. It is a real hospitality slogan, but it belongs
to Danny Meyer/USHG-adjacent training culture or to Ritz-Carlton-style empowerment programmes,
not to this book. **Do not attribute it to Guidara.** If you want the book's actual version of
"say yes first", the nearest verified passages are:

- Chapter 18's improvisation — Guidara committing to the hot dog *before* knowing how to plate it. **(a)**
- Chapter 17's **"What is the hospitality solution?"** (§8), which is the book's real "don't say no, get creative" doctrine. **(a)**

### 1.8 "The 2-minute check-in" — **NOT IN THE BOOK** **(a)**

No trace in the text or the index. This looks like a generic service-training device. The book's
actual check-in mechanics are:

- The **pre-meal** meeting, where stories and intel are shared before service. **(a)**
- The **captain's table visit** and the **parking-meter step of service** (§5). **(a)**
- **Bussing tables as a deliberate excuse to visit**: "Bussing tables showed the team I was there to help and gave me a way to check in with tables without worrying they'd ask a question I didn't have a good answer for." **(a)** — This one is genuinely useful for a teacher: he chose a task that *gave him a reason to be near people.*

### 1.9 "Small touches" — the book says **"special touches"** and **"Legend"** **(a)**

"Small touches" is not a named concept. The book's actual vocabulary is:

- **"these special touches"** — the words commoncog quotes from the text for the Legend system. **(a)**
- **"Legend"** — the internal shorthand, capitalised, for a bespoke guest moment. **(a)**
- **"Dreamweaver"** — the job title created to produce them. **(a)**

Recommendation: use **Legend** in the game. It is more specific, it is the book's own coinage,
and it gives you a mechanic-shaped word.

### 1.10 Intention — the frame that holds the rest **(a)**

Chapter 3:

> "Intention means every decision, from the most obviously significant to the seemingly mundane, matters. To do something with intentionality means to do it thoughtfully, with clear purpose and an eye on the desired result."

Plus his father's principle, which is the reason EMP could start at the top end: "It's easier to
learn the right way to do things at the high end than it is to break bad habits. You can always
take it down a notch later, but it's harder to go the other way." **(a)**

## 2. First impressions — what the book actually says

**A correction that will improve the game.** The book has *less* explicit "first ten seconds"
doctrine than its reputation suggests. Its most developed claim about timing is about the **end**
of an experience, not the start. Teaching a "first 10 seconds" rule as Guidara's would be
inventing. What he actually gives you is four things:

### 2.1 The end-of-meal asymmetry **(a)** — Chapter 17

> "If a stumble at the end of a meal can undo all the goodwill a restaurant has earned in the three hours preceding it, then a gorgeous, gracious gesture at the end can have the opposite effect. **(This is true in every service industry.)**"

That parenthetical is the most transferable sentence in the book for a school. It justifies
caring about the *last* minute of a lesson — the bell, the dismissal, the goodbye — at least as
much as the first.

### 2.2 The welcome must feel *different*, not merely excellent **(a)** — Chapter 17

This is his actual first-impression theory. After writing "Unreasonable Hospitality" on the
napkin, the team's specific goal was:

> "…to provide the kind of welcome that would give our guests the feeling we were doing things differently."

Crucially, he had already maxed out the *material* signals of quality and found them neutral:

> "…many of the luxurious details—the creamy linens, the thick leather cover on the wine list, the heft of the silver—were designed to communicate excellence."

His conclusion: those communicate *excellence*, and excellence is table stakes. They do not
communicate *welcome*. **(a)** for the quotes, **(c)** for the reading. This maps directly onto
your repo's `I-043` (thoughtfulness not luxury) — it is the book's own argument for it.

### 2.3 The first thing a guest sees is what you *didn't* do for them **(a)** — Chapter 16

The "earning informality" mechanism. Guests arriving at a four-star restaurant staffed by
people whose average dining-room age was **26** were alarmed. Guidara's response was
counter-intuitive:

> "…we had to earn the respect of guests alarmed by how young we were by amping up the formality at the beginning of their meal. Over the course of the evening, though, we'd earn their trust enough to shift their expectations… Our approach to service couldn't happen to them, it had to happen for them; we had to invite them, not force them."

And the principle, with the analogy:

> "When I started dating my wife, I called her dad Mr. Tosi; I knew I'd earned his trust when he finally told me to call him Gino. **Informality is something you earn.**"

**This is the single best scene in the book for Azizbek's first day.** He is 26-ish, new, and
Uzbek schoolchildren (and colleagues, and parents) will read a young male teacher as either a
pushover or a threat. Guidara's answer is not "be fun on day one". It is: *be more formal than
you want to be at the start, earn it, then relax.* That is a genuinely counter-intuitive,
teachable, and stageable choice — and it is the opposite of what a first-day game would
instinctively teach.

### 2.4 The first impression that isn't about you **(a)** — Chapter 18

> "There's nothing more flattering than a guest walking into the restaurant with luggage. It means they've chosen you to be either their first or last meal in New York—their first or last memory of the city. It's an enormous compliment, and a responsibility I don't take lightly."

A teacher's version: a child arriving on the first day has chosen nothing, but their *parents*
have — and the first day is the family's first memory of the year. **(c)** for the transfer, but
the book's framing ("first or last memory of the city") is **(a)**.

### 2.5 The Four Seasons, age twelve — the origin of his standard **(a)** — Chapter 2

His 12th birthday, at the Four Seasons, in a Brooks Brothers navy blazer with brass buttons:
duck carved tableside on a cart; he dropped his napkin and it was replaced with a fresh one, and
he was called **"sir"**. "The restaurant cast a spell… It put the world on pause." **(a)**

The lesson he draws is the famous line — "People will forget what you do; they'll forget what
you said. But they'll never forget how you made them feel." — with the honest footnote that it
is "often (but probably incorrectly) attributed to Maya Angelou." **(a)** If the game ever
quotes that line, use Guidara's own caveat. That is a nice small act of integrity to give a
teacher.

## 3. Learning about people — the machinery, which is the game's core mechanic

**This is the most important section for you.** Your game gates choices on `intel`. EMP
institutionalised exactly that, and gave it a job title. It was not a CRM; it was a **huddle and
a role**.

### 3.1 From one good moment to a repeatable system **(a)**

Chapter 18, right after the hot dog:

> "Athletes go to the tape when they've had a bad game, to see what they can fix. They don't often go to the tape when they've had a great game—but that's how you celebrate and hold on to what you did well. So I started talking to the staff in **pre-meal** about the hot dog: **What had made the gift so good? And what about it could we systemize?**"

That is the whole of `I-044` (systemise what is unique) in one sentence, including the method:
*deconstruct the win out loud, in front of the team, and extract the transferable part.*

`I-044` in your repo is therefore directly supported by book text, not by inference. Good.

### 3.2 "Legend" — the internal word **(a)**

From commoncog, quoting the book:

> "I told the story in pre-meal, and the term 'Legend' became shorthand within the restaurant for these special touches—as in, 'I did the best Legend for a t[able].'"

### 3.3 "Dreamweaver" — a person whose job is the mechanic **(a)**

Guidara created a new position at EMP: the **Dreamweaver** — people whose explicit job was to
create Legends. This is the strongest single fact in the whole brief for your game design. Your
`intel → choice` loop is not a game abstraction; it is a job description from a
four-star restaurant. The design lesson: **someone must own noticing.** If nobody's job is
noticing, noticing happens by accident.

### 3.4 What questions did staff actually ask? **(a)**

The one that is spelled out as a *step of service*, in Chapter 4, is about arrival:

> "The host would ask guests, 'How'd you get here tonight?' If they responded, 'Oh, we drove,' he'd follow up with, 'Cool! Where'd you park?' If they told him they were by a meter on the street, he asked which car was theirs so one of us could run out and drop a couple of quarters into the box while they were dining."

So: **a mundane, answerable, concrete question about the guest's evening** — not "how are you
today?" The chain is *question → fact → small physical act during the meal.* That is a three-beat
structure you can reuse for every lesson: ask, remember, act later.

### 3.5 The gift of the guest database story — and an honest "no" **(a)/(c)**

I looked specifically for a guest-preference database or CRM with names, birthdays and
allergies, because that is what most secondary summaries describe. **I did not verify one in
this book.** What I verified is different and better for you: the *mechanism* is **human** —
pre-meal meetings, storytelling, and a named role. The index does contain an entry for
**"'anchor'" (p.189)** which may relate to guest memory, but I could not read that page. **(a)**
for what's absent, **(c)** for what I think it means.

Practical consequence: if the game shows a teacher with a spreadsheet of 30 children's
preferences in week one, that is your invention, not Guidara's — and it is a weaker invention,
because a spreadsheet is not a social act. The book's version is that **you learn people by
talking to them and by telling each other what you learned.** That is more Uzbek-school-shaped
anyway: no budget, no software, one staffroom.

### 3.6 The Spago chair — learning by noticing an unmet need **(a)** — Chapter 18

One of Spago's best regulars ate lunch there five days a week. He was physically huge and
ordinary restaurant chairs hurt him. **Barbara Lazaroff** (Wolfgang Puck's then-wife) "asked the
regular's wife to secretly photograph and measure his favorite chair at home. She then had a
furniture maker replicate it and upholster the new chair in the house fabric."

Guidara's job was to move the enormous chair from the back of the restaurant to the table. Two
lessons: the intel came from **the person's family**, not from the person; and the gesture was
**never announced**. **(c)** for the second reading — but note that throughout the book the
Legends are *discovered*, not presented as favours.

### 3.7 Reconnaissance as a discipline **(a)** — Chapter 7

> "**Reconnaissance Matters**: You should never waste an opportunity to gather intel before your first day on the job."

He had Sam Lipp run an advance team. **(a)** This is your §10 bridge, and it justifies a
"gather intel before day one" game beat — but see the honesty note in §10: Guidara's
preparation was institutional, and a lone teacher can't run an advance team.

## 4. Gifts — what the book actually contains

### 4.1 The claim, in his own words **(a)** — Chapter 18

> "Gifts, to me, are deeply meaningful, which is why I get so mad when a business gives me a cheap tote with a branded USB drive. **Try harder! Do better!** Gifts are a way to tell people you saw, heard, and recognized them—that you cared enough to listen, and to do something with what you heard. A gift transforms an interaction, taking it from transactional to relational; there is no better way than a gift to demonstrate that someone is more than a customer or a line item on a spreadsheet. And the right one can help you to extend your hospitality all the way into someone's life."

And the axiom that should govern every gift choice in the game:

> "**The value of a gift isn't about what went into giving it, but how the person receiving it feels.** Maybe it was the thirtieth time we'd handed a traveling guest a snack box, but it was the first time for them—and their delight wasn't dimmed in the slightest because they hadn't been the only ones."

That is (a) and it is emphasis-marked in the book. It is also the answer to the obvious
objection — *"if I do the same nice thing for everyone, isn't it worthless?"* Guidara says no:
the recipient has not had it thirty times.

### 4.2 The verified gift inventory

| Gift | Detail | Ch. |
|---|---|---|
| **Street hot dog, plated four ways** | $2; from Abraham's Sabrett's cart; cut into four, mustard/ketchup swooshes, quenelles of sauerkraut and relish | 18 |
| **A custom chair** | photographed and measured in secret, replicated, upholstered in house fabric | 18 |
| **A full bottle of cognac with the check** | solved the end-of-meal problem; "the feeling of abundance was there" though few drank more than a sip | 17 |
| **Four sleds + a chauffeured SUV to Central Park** | for a family who had never seen snow | 5 |
| **Airplane snack boxes** | for travelling guests (index: pp. 212–13) | 18 |
| **Quarters in the parking meter** | a *step of service*, asked at the host stand | 4 |
| **Champagne rescued from a freezer + caviar + chocolates + an anniversary card** | Danny Meyer's story, adopted as EMP's standard | 4 |
| **Custom potato-chip bags / black-and-white cookies / chocolate-covered pretzels** | "a sense of place" — of, from and about New York | 20 |
| **A carafe of surf wax with a bow and a note** | Guidara's own *budget* version of the chair gesture | 18 |

The surf-wax example is the most useful one for a school, because he explicitly offers it as the
**cheap substitute**: if the custom gesture is "outside your budget, a can of surf wax on the
dash with a bow and a note will do much the same thing." **(a)**

### 4.3 Postcard? Granola? Birthday cake? Umbrella? — **not in the book** **(a)**

I searched for all four specifically. None of them appears as a Guidara gift example in the text
or index. Verdict:

- **Postcard** — not found. (The book has *notes* attached to gifts — "a bow and a note" — but no postcard.)
- **Granola** — not found. The travel gift is a **snack box**.
- **Umbrella** — not found.
- **Birthday cake** — appears, but **as a negative example**. Chapter 12: "These new-fashioned traditions are essential to a healthy culture, but—**ugh, birthday cake in the break room**—they tend not to stick unless people enjoy and look forward to them." **(a)**

That last one is a real find and worth a beat in the game: the book's only mention of birthday
cake is Guidara being *tired* of it. A generic cake is the archetype of a gift that costs money
and transmits nothing. That is a far better lesson than "give a birthday cake."

**If those four items are already in your chapter data, they are plausible teaching content but
they are not from this book.** Keep or cut them on their own merits; just don't source them to
Guidara.

### 4.4 The rule for choosing a gift **(a)** — Chapter 18

> "…you should always—always—be on the lookout for the Legend. Let's say a guy has come back to your dealership every couple of years for a new car, and you've gotten to know him well. When his kids go off to college, he starts looking at vans; with a little more time on his hands, he's rediscovered his adolescent passion for surfing."

The gift follows **a change in the person's life**, not a date in the calendar. For a school:
not "his birthday", but "his brother left for university", "he stopped playing football", "he
started sitting alone".

## 5. Personalised attention and noticing

### 5.1 Noticing is a trained behaviour, not a personality trait **(a)** — Chapter 11

> "It may not be possible to do everything perfectly, but it is possible to do many things perfectly. That's the very definition of excellence: getting as many details right as you can."

And the line that makes it a *habit* rather than an effort:

> "**The way you do one thing is the way you do everything.**"

He also cites Brailsford's "aggregation of marginal gains," and gives a startlingly concrete
example: at a rival dinner they noticed the blue tape on the floor had been **cut with scissors,
not torn**. **(a)** The detail is the message: someone chose.

### 5.2 Attention has a direction **(a)** — Chapter 4

> "Two things happen when the best leaders walk into a room. The people who work for them straighten up a little, making sure that everything's perfect—and they smile, too."

The test of a leader's attention is what happens to the room, not what the leader does. **(c)**
for the reading; the sentence is **(a)**.

### 5.3 Listen for the adjective, act on the noun **(c)**

Across the verified Legends the pattern is identical and worth naming explicitly in the game's
debrief: **the guest says something casually → someone hears it as data → a small physical thing
appears later in the same visit.** "The only thing we didn't eat was a hot dog" → hot dog before
the final savoury course. "We drove, we're on a meter" → quarters in the meter. "Never seen real
snow" → sleds. This is a mechanical, repeatable structure, and it is exactly your
`intel → gated choice → payoff` loop.

### 5.4 The cost of not noticing **(a)** — Chapter 6

The story of his own humiliation as a busboy at Spago: he opened a credenza and a stack of
butter plates slid out and shattered. "The crash was deafening, and the bustling restaurant fell
silent for a second or two. A few people clapped." Then the chef de cuisine came out and screamed
at him in front of colleagues and guests.

> "The memory of the shame and rage I felt that day will always be on my shoulder when I'm handling a mistake made by someone on my own team. I never forget how much impact—for good or for bad—a gesture by a leader can have."

Inattention in the other direction — a leader not noticing that a child is drowning — is the same
failure with a slower clock. **(c)** for the transfer; the memory is **(a)**.

### 5.5 "People want to be heard more than agreed with" **(a)** — Chapter 11

> "People usually want to be heard more than they want to be agreed with."

For a teacher this is the sentence that makes "noticing" cheap: you do not have to solve the
problem to have helped.

## 7. Monday morning: what a real teacher can actually do

Constraints honoured throughout: **no budget, no authority, 30+ children, colleagues who may
read enthusiasm as showing off, parents suspicious of a new male teacher.** Each item below is
either the book's own practice or a direct translation, marked accordingly.

### 7.1 The first day

1. **Be more formal than you want to be — then earn the informality.** **(a)** Guidara's own
   method with guests who didn't trust a 26-year-old staff. Walk in dressed properly, greet
   each child by the door with a handshake or a nod, use `siz` before you ever use `sen`, state
   the rules plainly, and *then* relax over the following weeks. Do not open with jokes to prove
   you're approachable; you'll spend a term recovering the authority you gave away.
   *Honest obstacle:* Uzbek colleagues may read early formality as coldness. Say the quiet part
   to one ally in the staffroom: "I'm strict for two weeks, then I loosen up — that's on purpose."
2. **Give them one thing they did not expect, in the first 45 minutes, that costs nothing.** **(c)**
   The hot dog's lesson is *cheap and specific*, not *expensive*. Something written by hand with
   their name on it beats anything printed. The book's own budget substitute for the big gesture
   is "a bow and a note." **(a)**
3. **Learn the room before you perform in it.** **(a)** "Reconnaissance Matters." Arrive 20
   minutes early, walk the room, look at the seats, the light, where the door bangs, where the
   tall boy will have to sit. Guidara's advance team did this for a restaurant; a teacher can do
   a low-cost version alone. *Honest limit:* he had staff; you have 20 minutes.

### 7.2 Learning 30 names and one fact each, fast

4. **Ask one mundane, answerable, concrete question — not "how are you?"** **(a)** The
   parking-meter chain: *"How did you get here today?" → "Where did you park?" → act later.*
   Classroom version: "What did you eat before you came?" / "Which way do you walk home?" These
   are answerable by a shy child, they are about the child's actual life, and they are useless
   as small talk — which is why they are remembered.
5. **Write the fact down immediately, and where the child can see you doing it.** **(c)** The
   book's mechanism is a *shared* record (pre-meal), not a secret file. A page in your planner is
   fine; a secret spreadsheet about children is a different and worse thing. If a child sees
   "Dinora — walks home past the bread shop — 3 brothers", that is a gift in itself.
6. **Reuse exactly one detail, out loud, within a week.** **(a)/(c)** This is the whole of
   hospitality compressed: *heard it → did something with it.* "How's your brother's leg?" The
   book is explicit that the gesture's size is irrelevant: "The value of a gift isn't about what
   went into giving it, but how the person receiving it feels."
7. **Teach a colleague one fact about one child.** **(a)** EMP's system was people telling each
   other in pre-meal. The cheapest version of that institution is a single sentence in the
   staffroom about one child. It costs 10 seconds, it makes you a colleague rather than a
   competitor, and it is the actual mechanism the book describes.

### 7.3 Making a room welcoming

8. **Fix the first five seconds, and the last five.** **(a)/(c)** Guidara's verified asymmetry:
   a stumble at the end can undo three hours of goodwill — *and a gracious gesture at the end can
   undo the stumble*. Stand at the door at the start; stand at the door at the end. Do not let
   the bell be the last thing that happens to them.
9. **Make one thing in the room deliberately, visibly not-standard.** **(a)** "…to provide the
   kind of welcome that would give our guests the feeling we were doing things differently."
   Excellence is invisible because everyone has it. One odd, warm, specific thing — a plant, a
   wall of one-line book recommendations in the children's handwriting — signals *this room is a
   person's room*.
10. **Do not buy things to signal quality.** **(a)** The creamy linens and thick leather wine
    list "were designed to communicate excellence" — and Guidara's conclusion was that they
    weren't the lever. This is your `I-043` and it is the book's own argument. At Sulton: spend
    nothing, and instead remove one thing that is ugly and no one chose.

### 7.4 Compliments and correction

11. **"Every person on the team should be hearing more about what they did well than what they
    could do better."** **(a)** Guidara attributes this to Christopher Russell's leadership
    teaching. The classroom version is a ratio, and ratios are countable: for one child you
    correct twice this week, name three specific things they did.
12. **Keep emotions out of criticism; correct in private; apologise for *how* you delivered it,
    not for the substance.** **(a)** All three are in Chapter 7. The last is the one nobody
    teaches: you do not withdraw the correction, you correct the delivery.
13. **Assume the best, especially when the behaviour is worst.** **(a)** "Make the charitable
    assumption." Ask "is everything okay?" before "why did you do that?" Guidara: maybe this
    person "needs more love and hospitality than anyone else in the room." **(a)**

### 7.5 Small gifts with no budget

14. **A note, not a purchase.** **(a)** The book's own cheap substitute is a note attached to a
    small thing. A handwritten line on the corner of their test paper outperforms anything you
    could buy, and it is the only version that scales to 30.
15. **Give the same good thing to everyone — and stop worrying that it's "not special".** **(a)**
    "Maybe it was the thirtieth time we'd handed a traveling guest a snack box, but it was the
    first time for them." This is the direct answer to the Uzbek-staffroom objection *"if you do
    it for one, you must do it for all."* Do it for all. It still works.
16. **Do not give the break-room cake.** **(a)** The book's only birthday cake is Guidara being
    tired of it: "ugh, birthday cake in the break room." A gift that requires no listening
    transmits no listening.

### 7.6 Remembering a detail and using it later

17. **Watch for the change, not the date.** **(a)** The surf-wax example: the gift follows a
    *life change* — kids leaving for college, rediscovering surfing. In a class of 30 the changes
    are visible: a brother leaves, a father goes to Russia for work, a boy stops coming to
    football. The gift is a sentence, not an object.
18. **Say the thing out loud when you use their detail.** **(c)** Much of the book's power comes
    from the guest *knowing* they were heard — Guidara "admitted to the guests that I'd been
    eavesdropping." An unacknowledged kindness is only half a kindness; the acknowledgement is
    what turns a transaction into a relationship.

### 7.7 The 5% for a teacher

19. **Count the 95% ruthlessly, then spend the 5% shamelessly.** **(a)** Concretely: your
    lessons are planned, your marking is on time, your register is accurate — that is the 95%.
    Then, once a term, do one thing that is disproportionate: the lesson that is entirely a
    game, the walk to the bread shop to measure a circle, the two hours you spend on one child's
    problem. **You cannot afford the 5% if you have not done the 95%**, and this is the discipline
    the book actually teaches.

## 8. The "wow" factor — memorable versus merely good

### 8.1 The definition of "unreasonable" **(a)** — Chapter 17

This is the most quotable and most mis-taught passage in the book, because the contrast is not
good-vs-bad:

> "Behavioral science expert Rory Sutherland says the opposite of a good idea should also be a good idea. That's why the idea of Unreasonable Hospitality was so compelling. **The opposite of Unreasonable Hospitality isn't treating people poorly, it's reasonable hospitality—a perfectly fine way of doing business.** But reasonable was not how we were going to become the number one restaurant in the world."

So the enemy is **competence**. That is a much harder and more interesting lesson than "be nice",
and it is exactly right for a school, where the enemy of a memorable lesson is a perfectly
adequate one.

### 8.2 The doctrine: "What is the hospitality solution?" **(a)** — Chapter 17

The operational instrument. When a problem arises, the reflex is a fallback position (a
comp, a discount, an apology). Guidara's alternative:

> "Imagine, though, that instead of resorting to one of these fallback positions, you asked yourself: **What is the hospitality solution?** What if you forced yourself to be creative, to develop a solution that worked because of—not in spite of—your dedication to generosity and extraordinary service?"

And the worked example — the end-of-meal problem, i.e. *the bill*:

> "…dropping off a full bottle of expensive booze at every table seemed like an unreasonably extravagant gesture, it was actually cost-effective. After an elaborate multicourse dinner (and usually plenty of wine), few people were interested in drinking more than a sip of that cognac. Yet the feeling of abundance was there."

With the line: "Please, help yourself to as much as you like, with our compliments. And when
you're ready, your check is right here." **(a)**

This is the single most transferable technique in the book for a teacher: **when you have to do
something unwelcome (a test, a telling-off, a phone call home), do not soften it — redesign it so
the generosity is part of it.** The cognac did not hide the bill. It sat next to it.

### 8.3 What makes it "unreasonable" rather than "extra" **(c)**

Reading across the verified Legends, four properties recur, and they are a usable checklist:

1. **Cheap relative to the effect.** A $2 hot dog outperformed "many, many thousands of dollars'
   worth of food." **(a)**
2. **Impossible to have planned from a script** — it came from something the guest said *tonight*. **(a)**
3. **Physically specific.** Cut into four. A swoosh of mustard. Quarters in a meter. Sleds. Never
   a voucher. **(a)**
4. **Acknowledged.** He told them he'd been eavesdropping. **(a)**

### 8.4 The gesture must be discovered, not announced **(a)/(c)**

Barbara Lazaroff had the chair made **secretly**. **(a)** Guidara never says "we do this for our
best guests." The Legends work because they arrive unrequested and unexplained. For a teacher
this is the difference between a kindness and a display: **do it, don't bill it.** If you tell
the class you stayed up late for them, you have converted a gift into an invoice. **(c)** for the
formulation; the behaviour is **(a)**.

### 8.5 "Speak things into existence" — the audacity clause **(a)** — Chapter 17

"We were fifty out of fifty" → he wrote *Unreasonable Hospitality* on a crumpled cocktail napkin
and opened the all-staff meeting with his father's favourite line: **"What would you attempt to
do if you knew you could not fail?"** He quotes Jay-Z — "I believe you can speak things into
existence" — and then: "I know this for sure: if you don't have the courage to state a goal out
loud, you'll never achieve it." **(a)**

The honest caveat, which the book itself supplies: "It was a tremendous risk to articulate that
dream out loud. When you set a goal for your team and fail to achieve it, you run the risk of
damaging morale." **(a)** Teach the risk, not just the triumph.

## 9. Teaching, mentoring, leading a team

### 9.1 The inward half — Sinek's framing **(a)**

From Sinek's letter: "he recognized that if he wanted his frontline teams to obsess about how
they made his customers feel, he had to obsess about how he made his employees feel. **The two
cannot be separated: great service cannot exist without great leadership.**" **(a)**

This is the hinge of the whole book and it is the answer to the cynical reading (see §9.7).
Hospitality that only flows outward is a performance and staff can feel it.

### 9.2 "Great leaders make leaders" **(a)** — Chapter 10

> "**Great leaders make leaders.** You don't want to have a hundred keys; you win when you end up with only one—the key to the front door."

And the method: "Tap into their passions then give them the keys." **(a)** Plus "Find the
win-win-win." **(a)**

For a teacher this reframes delegation: the point of giving a child a job is not to save
yourself work, it is that they become someone who can hold a key. **(c)** for the transfer.

### 9.3 "The best way to learn is to teach" **(a)** — Chapter 10

His father's line, and the study method that follows from it: study as if you will have to teach
it. **(a)** This is the book's most directly usable pedagogical principle, and it argues for
peer teaching in the game's classroom scenes — the struggling boy becomes the one who explains.

### 9.4 Excellence as an accumulation of details **(a)** — Chapter 11

> "**Excellence Is the Culmination of Thousands of Details Executed Perfectly.**"

And the humane qualification: "It may not be possible to do everything perfectly, but it is
possible to do many things perfectly. That's the very definition of excellence: getting as many
details right as you can." **(a)** Two more from the same chapter worth teaching: traffic in a
dining room should move like ballet, not football; and "Go too fast and you will end up slowing
the whole restaurant down." **(a)**

### 9.5 Correction, and what a leader's temper costs **(a)** — Chapters 6 and 7

The screaming chef at Spago (§5.4) is the book's negative model, and Guidara says he carries the
memory "always… on my shoulder" when handling his own team's mistakes. **(a)** The positive
model is Christopher Russell's speech, quoted in Chapter 7:

> "I'm also clear about what my job is, which is to do what's best for the restaurant, not to do what's best for any of you. More often than not, what's best for the restaurant will include doing what's best for you. But the only way I can take care of all of you as individuals is by always putting the restaurant first."

That is a hard, unsentimental sentence, and it is the correct antidote to "we're a family" —
which the book uses carefully. In Chapter 19: "**If you call your team your family, you need to
invest in them**" — and he acted on it, opening EMP Summer House in the Hamptons so that no
staff were lost during the renovation: "without our team, the restaurant was just four walls,
some tables, and a stove." **(a)**

### 9.6 Sustaining people through failure **(a)** — Chapters 12, 14, 15

- **Let them grieve.** "After a setback, I'd tell the team to go ahead and wallow… Let's allow ourselves to feel the disappointment; it's real and we don't need to pretend it's not." **(a)**
- **Mark the loss, don't skip it.** They threw their first party for themselves on a night they *lost* — the Beard Rising Star, to David Chang, May 2007. Daniel Boulud came downtown and made scrambled eggs. **(a)**
- **"Drink your best bottle not on your best day but on your worst."** **(a)**
- **Look down, not just ahead.** The cook who sprinted in at 11 p.m. believing she was two hours late, when she was ten hours early — "not enough is written about how leaders also need to have the awareness to look down, to see what's really beneath their feet." **(a)**
- **"Put your own oxygen mask on first before assisting others."** "Slow down to speed up." **(a)**
- **Systemise asking for help.** He put up a sign; "systemizing it stripped the stigma from it." **(a)** For a staffroom, this is the most practical item in the book: make asking for help a *procedure*, and the shame goes out of it.

### 9.7 The honest objection — read this before you build a level on §9

A widely-read Goodreads review of the book makes the criticism that the book never discusses
**compensation**, and that its "anyone can do this" framing understates the advantages Guidara
had (a father in the industry, Cornell, Danny Meyer's backing, a four-star restaurant's margins).
**(b)** — this is a reviewer's argument, not a fact, and I am citing it as the strongest version
of the sceptical case rather than endorsing it.

Why it matters for your game: a teacher cannot buy sleds, cannot close the school for a retreat,
and cannot pay an exhausted colleague more. If the game's lesson is "be generous like Guidara",
an Uzbek teacher will correctly conclude it is not for him. **The version that survives contact
with Sulton is the 95/5 discipline plus the $2 hot dog** — the cheap, specific, discovered
gesture — and the honest admission that the big-budget half of the book is not available to him.
Put that admission in the debrief.

On headcount: the book references a large team and the index and text mention roughly 200 staff
at the EMP/NoMad/Make It Nice group, but **I did not verify an exact figure** and would not state
one. **(a)** for "large multi-restaurant group", **(c)** for any specific number.

## 10. Being a newcomer, and earning credibility fast

### 10.1 The verified facts of his arrival **(a)/(b)/(c)**

- Danny Meyer asked him to become general manager of Eleven Madison Park — the offer is described in Chapter 6, and Guidara's reaction was not immediate acceptance (the chapter ends on "Which begs the question: Why on earth did I say no?"). **(a)**
- **He was 26.** This is **(b)** widely reported; the arithmetic supports it — in Chapter 16 he says "I was twenty-nine" when EMP got its fourth star in 2009, which puts his birth around 1980 and his EMP GM appointment around 2006. **(c)** for the arithmetic.
- The team he inherited was older and more experienced than he was in places: at the four-star moment the average dining-room age was 26, Daniel Humm was 32, Guidara 29 and "still getting carded in bars." Guests were visibly alarmed by how young the staff were. **(a)**
- He was not a stranger to the company — he had worked at Tabla, the Modern, and had turned down Blue Smoke. His credibility was *internal* before it was external. **(a)**

### 10.2 What actually earned him credibility **(a)** — and it is not charisma

1. **He did the unglamorous work in front of the team.** "I bussed a lot of tables when I was
   general manager… Bussing tables showed the team I was there to help." **(a)** A new teacher
   who picks up litter, hands back marked books on time, and covers a colleague's duty earns
   more than one who gives a good speech.
2. **He did a trail in the kitchen and was humiliated on purpose.** At Tabla he deveined shrimp
   for three hours and had his onion thrown in the bin by Floyd Cardoz. **(a)** The point was not
   skill; it was that the kitchen saw him try and fail at their work. **(c)** for the reading.
3. **He gathered intel before he arrived.** "Reconnaissance Matters… never waste an opportunity
   to gather intel before your first day on the job." **(a)**
4. **He named a direction and asked people to choose it.** "We're going to make the kind of place
   we want to eat at… **That's where we're going. Will you come?**" **(a)** Not "follow me" —
   *will you come*. That is the sentence to steal for Azizbek's first staffroom scene.
5. **He argued with his boss, twice, on principle.** On Relais & Châteaux he asked Danny "Are
   you telling me we can't apply? Or that we shouldn't?" and Danny's reply was "the fact that
   you're back here challenging me tells me you know something I don't." **(a)** Credibility
   with a superior came from *being specific and returning with the question*, not from
   deference.
6. **He was willing to fail publicly.** "You don't reach the top by taking no for an answer,
   especially not the first time you get it. We needed to be willing to fail." **(a)**

### 10.3 The line that should open the game **(a)** — Chapter 19

> "**Nobody Knows What They're Doing Before They Do It.**"

That is a chapter title. For a first-day teacher it is the single most relieving sentence in the
book, and it is *not* a platitude in context — it sits next to the story of Danny Meyer offering
to sell him the restaurant, i.e. the most experienced person in the room also improvising. **(a)**

### 10.4 The counter-intuitive newcomer move **(a)** — Chapter 16

Restated because it is the answer to §10: **do not try to be liked into credibility.** "Amping
up the formality at the beginning of their meal" was how a 26-year-old earned the right to be
informal later. **Informality is something you earn.** **(a)**

# THE 12 STRONGEST TEACHABLE IDEAS

Ranked by how much a teacher can *do* with them on a Monday, and by how strongly the book
supports them. Each has the idea, the book's evidence, and one concrete Sulton application.

---

**1. "Being present" beats "being attentive".**
*Evidence (a):* the Tribeca Grill tip sheet — the servers who saw everything and turned tables
fastest made *less* money than the ones who were slower and fully with the guest. "Caring so much
about what you're doing that you stop caring about everything you need to do next." "The first
group was attentive; the second paid attention."
*Sulton, Monday:* in one lesson this week, sit down at a desk next to a child and stay for 90
seconds with no other target. Do not scan the room. Then notice what you failed to do while you
were there — that's the gap between the two words.

---

**2. Systemise the good moment — deconstruct your own wins out loud.**
*Evidence (a):* "Athletes go to the tape when they've had a bad game… They don't often go to the
tape when they've had a great game." He put the hot dog on the pre-meal agenda with one question:
"What had made the gift so good? And what about it could we systemize?"
*Sulton, Monday:* at the end of each week, write three lines on one lesson that worked: *what
exactly happened, why did it land, what part of it can I repeat without the same children?* That
is a pre-meal of one.

---

**3. The 95/5 rule: count the pennies, then spend the 5% foolishly.**
*Evidence (a):* "Manage 95% of your business down to the penny and the other 5% foolishly." Four
sleds and a chauffeured SUV to Central Park for a family who had never seen snow.
*Sulton, Monday:* be ruthlessly punctual and organised for a fortnight — books marked, register
correct, lesson started on the minute. Then, once, spend a disproportionate hour on one child or
one lesson. The 5% is only affordable *after* the 95%, and this is the whole discipline.

---

**4. Earn the informality — start more formal than you want to be.**
*Evidence (a):* the four-star dining room staffed by 26-year-olds who alarmed guests; "we had to
earn the respect of guests alarmed by how young we were by amping up the formality at the
beginning of their meal… Our approach to service couldn't happen to them, it had to happen for
them." And: "I called her dad Mr. Tosi; I knew I'd earned his trust when he finally told me to
call him Gino. Informality is something you earn."
*Sulton, Monday:* week one, shirt tucked in, `siz`, rules stated plainly, no jokes at a child's
expense. From week three, start using one with them. Say to one ally in the staffroom that the
strictness is a plan, not a mood.

---

**5. Ask one concrete question, then do something with the answer.**
*Evidence (a):* the host's "How'd you get here tonight?" → "Where'd you park?" → someone runs out
with quarters during dinner. Guidara on gifts: "that you cared enough to listen, **and to do
something with what you heard**."
*Sulton, Monday:* "Which way do you walk home?" Write it down. Within a week, mention the bread
shop they pass. Two of those a day is 30 children in three weeks.

---

**6. Find the Legend — watch for the change, not the date.**
*Evidence (a):* the car-dealership example — the customer's kids leave for college, he starts
looking at vans, he rediscovers surfing → surf wax on the dash with a bow and a note. And the
Spago chair: the intel came from the man's *wife*.
*Sulton, Monday:* list three children whose circumstances just changed — a sibling left, a parent
went abroad for work, they stopped coming to football. One sentence to each, this week. It costs
nothing and it is the book's actual method.

---

**7. The opposite of great hospitality is not bad hospitality — it's reasonable hospitality.**
*Evidence (a):* "The opposite of Unreasonable Hospitality isn't treating people poorly, it's
reasonable hospitality—a perfectly fine way of doing business." Rory Sutherland: the opposite of
a good idea should also be a good idea.
*Sulton, Monday:* take the lesson you are most satisfied with and ask one question: *what would
the reasonable version of this lesson be?* Then do one thing the reasonable version wouldn't.
That is the entire creative discipline in a single question.

---

**8. "What is the hospitality solution?" — redesign the unwelcome thing instead of softening it.**
*Evidence (a):* when the check had to arrive, the fallback was to apologise; the hospitality
solution was a full bottle of cognac on the table — "cost-effective… yet the feeling of abundance
was there." He did not hide the bill; he sat the generosity next to it.
*Sulton, Monday:* the thing you dread — a test, a phone call home, a telling-off. Do not soften
it. Add a genuine, specific generosity beside it: a written note on the test paper naming one
thing they actually did well, delivered with the result. Both arrive together.

---

**9. The end of the experience carries disproportionate weight.**
*Evidence (a):* "If a stumble at the end of a meal can undo all the goodwill a restaurant has
earned in the three hours preceding it, then a gorgeous, gracious gesture at the end can have the
opposite effect. (This is true in every service industry.)"
*Sulton, Monday:* never let the bell be the last thing that happens. Thirty seconds at the door
at the end of the lesson — a word to two children, by name, about that lesson. It is the cheapest
hospitality there is, and the book's own theory says it is the highest-leverage.

---

**10. Assume the best, especially when the behaviour is worst.**
*Evidence (a):* "Make the charitable assumption." Assume the best in people especially when they
aren't behaving well; ask "is everything okay?" first. Maybe this person needs more hospitality
than anyone else in the room.
*Sulton, Monday:* the next time a child is rude, the first sentence is "Is everything all right?"
— not "Why did you do that?" Keep the consequence; change the opening. And apologise for *how*
you delivered a correction, never for the correction itself.

---

**11. Make leaders, not followers — give away the keys.**
*Evidence (a):* "Great leaders make leaders. You don't want to have a hundred keys; you win when
you end up with only one—the key to the front door." "Tap into their passions then give them the
keys." His father: "the best way to learn is to teach."
*Sulton, Monday:* pick the child who is worst at maths and ask them to teach next lesson's first
five minutes to the class — after you've taught them it privately. Not a reward for the strong.
The book's logic says the *teaching* is the learning.

---

**12. Nobody knows what they're doing before they do it — and the repair is a stated direction.**
*Evidence (a):* "Nobody Knows What They're Doing Before They Do It." And from the mission
meeting: "We're going to make the kind of place we want to eat at… That's where we're going.
**Will you come?**"
*Sulton, Monday:* to the class on day one, say one true sentence about what this year is going to
be — and then ask them. Not "do you understand the rules", but "will you come?" Thirty children
will forgive a teacher who doesn't yet know how to do it, if he has said where he is going.

**Two design notes that fall out of the research.**

1. **"Dreamweaver" is a better frame for the game's mechanic than "intel".** EMP created a job
   whose purpose was to notice. A chapter where Azizbek is *given* that job — by a colleague, a
   head teacher, or self-appointed — turns your `intel → gated choice` system into something the
   book literally did, rather than a game abstraction.
2. **The debrief should say the uncomfortable part.** The book's most expensive gestures are not
   available to an Uzbek teacher. The teachable core is the $2 hot dog and the parking meter, and
   the reason they worked is that someone was *listening* — not that someone was rich.
